3 · The ellipse

How far away the moon was

Path during TishreiHoshana Rabbah morning

The moon's orbit is an ellipse with Earth at one focus. At its true proportions it is almost a circle: the nearest point (perigee) is about 13% closer than the farthest (apogee). The dots trace where the moon was each day of Tishrei in the selected year; the large dot is the pre-dawn hour of Hoshana Rabbah.

The shape does not change the rising time much. What moves moonrise from year to year is the moon's north-south drift and the angle of its path to the horizon (section 2). Distance mostly changes how big and bright the moon looks.

Drag the height slider to see which years had the moon at that height at the same time relative to alos. "Set by hand" moves only the moon's height, keeping everything else about the chosen moment, so you can see the angle's effect on its own. The night-long chart always uses the real heights.

Why the moon's height matters: τ is fixed by the cloud, but light coming in at a slant crosses more of it. The path is τ ÷ sin(height). A moon 65° up crosses 1.1 times the layer; at 30° it crosses 2 times; at 10° almost 6 times. The same cloud that only dims a high moon can hide a low one completely.

Model: direct beam transmitted as e−τ/μ (μ is the sine of the moon's height). Cloud reflectance R = [(1 − g)τ + (2/3 − μ)(1 − e−τ/μ)] ÷ [4/3 + (1 − g)τ] (two-stream, no absorption), transmission 1 − R, asymmetry g = 0.85. The forward glow around the moon widens as τ grows, because each scattering spreads the light further, until the layer glows evenly. The rendering is a sketch of that physics, not a photograph.

For teaching

Clock hours and the hours of that night

Every hour and minute on this page is clock time. Gaps like "moonrise 2h 56m after sunset" or "an hour before alos" are given so a reader today can follow the astronomy. They are not how the people in Mainz experienced that night.

They reckoned in sha'os zmaniyos, seasonal hours. By day those can be read from the sun, with a sundial. At night there is no sun to read, so the hour has to be judged by other means: the stars, the moon, candles, the length of the learning. All of these are far harder and less exact than a sundial.

So when this page says the moon rose 1h 35m after sunset in one year and 4h 31m in another, that is a precision nobody that night had. For them it was a judgment made by eye in the dark, and the text shows the final call was made by looking at the sky: היו סבורים שעלה עמוד השחר, "they thought dawn had risen."
